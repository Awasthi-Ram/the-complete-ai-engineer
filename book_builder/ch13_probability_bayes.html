1. The Mathematics of Uncertainty

Deterministic software engineering operates in boolean binaries: a condition is either True or False, a pointer is either valid or null. Machine learning operates in continuous probability spaces: an autonomous vehicle never says "the obstacle ahead is definitely a stop sign"; it calculates a posterior probability distribution $P(\text{StopSign} \mid \text{Lidar}, \text{Camera}) = 0.987$. When dealing with sensor noise, missing features, and inherently stochastic real-world phenomena, probability is the universal mathematical grammar of artificial intelligence.

Kolmogorov's Axioms of Probability

Given a sample space $\Omega$ of all possible mutually exclusive elementary outcomes, an event space $\mathcal{F}$ of measurable subsets of $\Omega$, a valid probability measure $P: \mathcal{F} \to [0, 1]$ must satisfy three fundamental axioms formulated by Andrey Kolmogorov in 1933:

  1. Non-negativity: For any event $A \in \mathcal{F}$, $P(A) \ge 0$.
  2. Unit Measure: The probability of the entire sample space occurring is unity: $P(\Omega) = 1$.
  3. Countable Additivity: For any countable sequence of pairwise disjoint events ($A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset$ for all $i \neq j$): $$P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^\infty A_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty P(A_i)$$

Joint, Marginal, and Conditional Probability

From these axioms arise the core operational rules governing multidimensional random variables:

  • Joint Probability $P(X = x, Y = y)$: The probability that random variable $X$ takes value $x$ AND random variable $Y$ takes value $y$ simultaneously.
  • Marginal Probability (Sum Rule): Summing (or integrating) out irrelevant nuisance variables from a joint distribution: $$P(X = x) = \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} P(X = x, Y = y) \quad \text{(Discrete)}, \qquad P(X = x) = \int_{\mathcal{Y}} P(X = x, Y = y) \, dy \quad \text{(Continuous)}$$
  • Conditional Probability (Product Rule): The probability of event $A$ given that event $B$ has definitively occurred ($P(B) > 0$): $$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \implies P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B) P(B)$$
  • Chain Rule of Probability: Factoring any high-dimensional joint probability distribution into a cascade of univariate conditionals: $$P(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n) = P(X_1) \prod_{i=2}^n P(X_i \mid X_1, \dots, X_{i-1})$$ This exact factorization forms the mathematical foundation of Auto-regressive Large Language Models (GPT, Claude, Llama), where the probability of a document is the product of token probabilities conditioned on preceding tokens!
  • Independence vs. Conditional Independence: Two variables $X$ and $Y$ are unconditionally independent ($X \perp Y$) if $P(X, Y) = P(X)P(Y)$. They are conditionally independent given $Z$ ($X \perp Y \mid Z$) if: $$P(X, Y \mid Z) = P(X \mid Z) P(Y \mid Z)$$ Conditional independence is the cornerstone of Graphical Models, Naive Bayes classifiers, and Markov Random Fields.

2. Expectation, Moments & Variance Mechanics

Probability distributions are high-dimensional objects. In practice, machine learning models optimize statistical summaries (moments) of these distributions.

The Expectation Operator ($\mathbb{E}$)

The expected value of a function $g(X)$ with respect to probability distribution $P(X)$ represents the probability-weighted average:

$$\mathbb{E}_{X \sim P}[g(X)] = \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} g(x) P(x) \quad \text{(Discrete)}, \qquad \mathbb{E}_{X \sim P}[g(X)] = \int_{\Omega} g(x) P(x) \, dx \quad \text{(Continuous)}$$

Crucial Properties of Expectation:

  1. Linearity of Expectation: For any random variables $X$ and $Y$ (regardless of whether they are independent or correlated) and constants $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$: $$\mathbb{E}[aX + bY] = a\mathbb{E}[X] + b\mathbb{E}[Y]$$
  2. Expectation of Independent Variables: If $X \perp Y$, then $\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X] \mathbb{E}[Y]$.
  3. Law of Total Expectation (Tower Property): $$\mathbb{E}[Y] = \mathbb{E}_{X}\left[ \mathbb{E}_{Y \mid X}[Y \mid X] \right]$$

Variance, Covariance, and the Correlation Matrix

Variance quantifies the expected squared dispersion of a random variable around its mean $\mu = \mathbb{E}[X]$:

$$\text{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\left[ (X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2 \right] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2$$

Under linear scaling: $\text{Var}(aX + b) = a^2 \text{Var}(X)$. Adding a constant does not change spread; scaling by $a$ scales variance quadratically.

For two random variables $X$ and $Y$, the Covariance measures their linear co-movement:

$$\text{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}\left[ (X - \mathbb{E}[X])(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y]) \right] = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$$

Notice that $\text{Cov}(X, X) = \text{Var}(X)$. If $X \perp Y$, then $\text{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$. (Warning: zero covariance implies independence only if the variables are jointly Gaussian; in general non-linear cases, $\text{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$ does not imply independence!).

The Pearson Correlation Coefficient normalizes covariance by the product of standard deviations, yielding a scale-invariant metric in $[-1, 1]$:

$$\rho_{XY} = \frac{\text{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y} = \frac{\text{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sqrt{\text{Var}(X)\text{Var}(Y)}}$$

The Law of Total Variance (Eve's Law): In hierarchical models and reinforcement learning, total variance decomposes into unexplained variance and explained variance: $$\text{Var}(Y) = \mathbb{E}_X\left[ \text{Var}(Y \mid X) \right] + \text{Var}_X\left( \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X] \right)$$

Geometry of the Multivariate Normal: The Mahalanobis Distance

In a Multivariate Normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})$, the covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is symmetric positive semi-definite ($\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^T$ and $\mathbf{z}^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{z} \ge 0$). By the Spectral Theorem, $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ decomposes as:

$$\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \mathbf{U} \boldsymbol{\Lambda} \mathbf{U}^T = \sum_{i=1}^d \lambda_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{u}_i^T$$

Contours of constant probability density form hyper-ellipsoids centered at $\boldsymbol{\mu}$. The eigenvectors $\mathbf{u}_i$ define the principal axes of the ellipsoid, and the square roots of the eigenvalues $\sqrt{\lambda_i}$ define the lengths of the semi-axes (standard deviations along those orthogonal directions).

The term inside the exponent is the squared Mahalanobis Distance:

$$D_M^2(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\mu}) = (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^T \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})$$

Unlike standard Euclidean distance $\|\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}\|_2^2$, the Mahalanobis distance scales distances inversely by the variance in each direction and accounts for correlated features, making it indispensable for anomaly detection.

4. Bayesian Inference & The Four Pillars of Bayes

Bayes' Theorem converts a generative prior belief into an updated posterior belief after assimilating empirical data:

🏆 Bayes' Theorem Dissected for Machine Learning

$$P(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \mathcal{D}) = \frac{P(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) P(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{P(\mathcal{D})} = \frac{P(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) P(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\int_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}} P(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}') P(\boldsymbol{\theta}') \, d\boldsymbol{\theta}'}$$
  • $P(\boldsymbol{\theta})$: The Prior. Encapsulates our domain knowledge or regularizing inductive bias about parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ before observing data $\mathcal{D}$.
  • $P(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta})$: The Likelihood. The probability of observing dataset $\mathcal{D}$ if the true underlying parameters were $\boldsymbol{\theta}$. Under i.i.d. sampling, $P(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \prod_{i=1}^N P(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \boldsymbol{\theta})$.
  • $P(\mathcal{D}) = \int P(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}') P(\boldsymbol{\theta}') d\boldsymbol{\theta}'$: The Evidence (Marginal Likelihood). The normalizing constant ensuring the posterior integrates to 1. In deep learning with millions of parameters, computing this integral over $\mathbb{R}^{10^9}$ is intractable, necessitating MCMC or Variational Inference.
  • $P(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \mathcal{D})$: The Posterior Distribution. Our complete, uncertainty-aware updated belief over parameter space given the empirical evidence.

5. Estimation Theory: MLE, MAP & The Loss Function Bridge

In deep learning, we frequently summarize the posterior distribution with a single optimal point estimate $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$. The two primary point-estimation frameworks are:

1. Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)

MLE seeks the parameter vector $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{MLE}}$ that maximizes the probability of generating the observed training dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_N\}$:

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{MLE}} = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \prod_{i=1}^N P(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \sum_{i=1}^N \ln P(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \left[ -\sum_{i=1}^N \ln P(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) \right]$$

Minimizing Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL) is the foundational objective of supervised deep learning.

2. Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) Estimation

MAP incorporates prior beliefs $P(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ over parameters, finding the mode of the posterior distribution:

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} P(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \mathcal{D}) = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \frac{P(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) P(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{P(\mathcal{D})}$$

Since the evidence $P(\mathcal{D})$ does not depend on $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, we drop it and take the natural log:

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \left[ \ln P(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) + \ln P(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \right] = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \left[ -\sum_{i=1}^N \ln P(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) - \ln P(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \right]$$

The Great Mathematical Bridge of AI:

  • Gaussian Noise $\implies$ Mean Squared Error (MSE): Assuming $y = f(\mathbf{x}) + \epsilon$ with $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$, NLL is identically MSE.
  • Bernoulli Likelihood $\implies$ Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE): Assuming $y \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p)$, NLL is identically BCE loss.
  • Categorical Likelihood $\implies$ Multi-Class Categorical Cross-Entropy: Softmax + NLL is the cross-entropy loss used to train all neural language and vision classifiers.
  • Gaussian Prior on Weights $\implies L_2$ Regularization (Ridge / Weight Decay): If $P(\mathbf{w}) \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma_w^2 \mathbf{I})$, $-\ln P(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{2\sigma_w^2} \|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2 + \text{const}$. MAP directly yields $L_2$ weight decay with $\lambda = \frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma_w^2}$!
  • Laplace Prior on Weights $\implies L_1$ Regularization (Lasso): If $P(\mathbf{w}) \propto \exp(-\frac{\|\mathbf{w}\|_1}{b})$, $-\ln P(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{b} \|\mathbf{w}\|_1 + \text{const}$. The non-differentiable sharp peak at zero forces weights to exact numerical zero, producing sparse models!
Loss functions and regularizers are not empirical heuristics; they are the exact mathematical consequence of probabilistic likelihoods and priors!

6. Complete Mathematical Derivation: Gaussian MLE & Bessel's Bias Correction

Let $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_N \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$. We derive the Maximum Likelihood Estimators for both $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$, and prove whether they are biased or unbiased.

Derivation Step 1: Write the Joint Log-Likelihood

$$L(\mu, \sigma^2) = \prod_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{(x_i - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right)$$ $$\ell(\mu, \sigma^2) = \ln L = -\frac{N}{2} \ln(2\pi) - \frac{N}{2} \ln(\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \mu)^2$$

Derivation Step 2: Solve for $\hat{\mu}_{\text{MLE}}$

Differentiate $\ell$ with respect to $\mu$ and set to zero:

$$\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mu} = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_{i=1}^N 2(x_i - \mu)(-1) = \frac{1}{\sigma^2} \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \mu) = 0$$ $$\sum_{i=1}^N x_i - N\mu = 0 \implies \hat{\mu}_{\text{MLE}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N x_i = \bar{x}$$

Is $\hat{\mu}_{\text{MLE}}$ unbiased? Check its expectation:

$$\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mu}_{\text{MLE}}] = \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N x_i \right] = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{E}[x_i] = \frac{1}{N}(N\mu) = \mu$$

Since $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mu}_{\text{MLE}}] = \mu$, the sample mean is an unbiased estimator.

Derivation Step 3: Solve for $\hat{\sigma}^2_{\text{MLE}}$

Differentiate $\ell$ with respect to $v = \sigma^2$ and set to zero:

$$\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial v} = -\frac{N}{2v} + \frac{1}{2v^2} \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \mu)^2 = 0 \implies -\frac{N}{2v} + \frac{1}{2v^2} \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \hat{\mu})^2 = 0$$ $$\frac{N}{2v} = \frac{1}{2v^2} \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \hat{\mu})^2 \implies \hat{\sigma}^2_{\text{MLE}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar{x})^2$$

Derivation Step 4: Prove the Bias of $\hat{\sigma}^2_{\text{MLE}}$ (Bessel's Correction)

Compute the expected value of $\hat{\sigma}^2_{\text{MLE}}$:

$$\mathbb{E}[\hat{\sigma}^2_{\text{MLE}}] = \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar{x})^2 \right] = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{E}[(x_i - \bar{x})^2]$$

Expand $(x_i - \bar{x}) = (x_i - \mu) - (\bar{x} - \mu)$:

$$(x_i - \bar{x})^2 = (x_i - \mu)^2 - 2(x_i - \mu)(\bar{x} - \mu) + (\bar{x} - \mu)^2$$ $$\sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar{x})^2 = \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \mu)^2 - 2(\bar{x} - \mu) \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \mu) + N(\bar{x} - \mu)^2$$

Since $\sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \mu) = N(\bar{x} - \mu)$, this simplifies to:

$$\sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar{x})^2 = \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \mu)^2 - N(\bar{x} - \mu)^2$$

Taking expectations:

$$\mathbb{E}\left[ \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar{x})^2 \right] = \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{E}[(x_i - \mu)^2] - N \mathbb{E}[(\bar{x} - \mu)^2]$$

Recall that $\mathbb{E}[(x_i - \mu)^2] = \text{Var}(x_i) = \sigma^2$, and $\mathbb{E}[(\bar{x} - \mu)^2] = \text{Var}(\bar{x}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{N}$:

$$\mathbb{E}\left[ \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar{x})^2 \right] = N\sigma^2 - N\left(\frac{\sigma^2}{N}\right) = N\sigma^2 - \sigma^2 = (N-1)\sigma^2$$

Dividing by $N$:

$$\mathbb{E}[\hat{\sigma}^2_{\text{MLE}}] = \frac{N-1}{N} \sigma^2$$

Conclusion: The MLE variance estimator systematically underestimates the true population variance by a factor of $\frac{N-1}{N}$ because the deviations are measured relative to the empirical sample mean $\bar{x}$ rather than the true unknown mean $\mu$ (consuming 1 degree of freedom). To obtain an unbiased estimator, we multiply by $\frac{N}{N-1}$, yielding Bessel's Correction:

$$s^2 = \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar{x})^2 \implies \mathbb{E}[s^2] = \sigma^2$$

Problem 1.3.1 Easy Google Meta

QUESTION: A production payment network experiences fraudulent transactions at a base rate of $P(F) = 0.002$ ($0.2\%$). An automated AI fraud detector has a True Positive Rate (Sensitivity) of $P(+ \mid F) = 0.98$ and a False Positive Rate of $P(+ \mid \neg F) = 0.02$.

  1. A transaction flags positive ($+$). Compute the exact posterior probability $P(F \mid +)$ that the transaction is genuinely fraudulent.
  2. Suppose a secondary independent verification model also tests positive ($+_2$) on this flagged transaction, with identical sensitivity $0.98$ and false positive rate $0.02$. Compute the updated posterior probability $P(F \mid +, +_2)$.
✓ Complete Step-by-Step Numerical Solution

Step 1: Compute Posterior after First Positive Test:
By Bayes' Theorem: $$P(F \mid +) = \frac{P(+ \mid F) P(F)}{P(+)}$$ Expand denominator $P(+)$ using the Law of Total Probability ($P(\neg F) = 1 - 0.002 = 0.998$): $$P(+) = P(+ \mid F) P(F) + P(+ \mid \neg F) P(\neg F)$$ $$P(+) = (0.98 \times 0.002) + (0.02 \times 0.998) = 0.00196 + 0.01996 = 0.02192$$ Calculate posterior: $$P(F \mid +) = \frac{0.00196}{0.02192} \approx 0.089416 = 8.94\%$$ Insight: Despite a $98\%$ accurate test, an alarm is only genuine $\approx 8.94\%$ of the time because the base rate is so small ($1$ in $500$).

Step 2: Sequential Bayesian Updating after Second Positive Test:
In Bayesian inference, the posterior from the first test becomes the prior for the second test: $$P_{\text{new}}(F) = P(F \mid +) = 0.089416, \quad P_{\text{new}}(\neg F) = 1 - 0.089416 = 0.910584$$ Compute the new marginal probability of testing positive $P(+_2)$: $$P(+_2) = P(+_2 \mid F) P_{\text{new}}(F) + P(+_2 \mid \neg F) P_{\text{new}}(\neg F)$$ $$P(+_2) = (0.98 \times 0.089416) + (0.02 \times 0.910584) = 0.087628 + 0.018212 = 0.105840$$ Compute the updated posterior: $$P(F \mid +, +_2) = \frac{0.087628}{0.105840} \approx 0.8279 = 82.79\%$$ Result: Two consecutive independent positive tests skyrocket the confidence from $8.94\%$ to $82.79\%$!

Problem 1.3.2 Easy Apple

QUESTION: Given a dataset of $N=4$ two-dimensional feature points: $$\mathbf{x}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{x}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{x}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{x}_4 = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix}$$ Compute step-by-step:

  1. The sample mean vector $\bar{\mathbf{x}} = [\bar{x}_1, \bar{x}_2]^T$.
  2. The unbiased sample covariance matrix $\mathbf{S} = \begin{bmatrix} s_{11} & s_{12} \\ s_{21} & s_{22} \end{bmatrix}$ (using $N-1$ denominator).
  3. The Pearson correlation coefficient $r_{12}$.
✓ Complete Step-by-Step Numerical Solution

Step 1: Compute Sample Mean Vector: $$\bar{x}_1 = \frac{1 + 2 + 3 + 4}{4} = \frac{10}{4} = 2.5$$ $$\bar{x}_2 = \frac{2 + 4 + 5 + 9}{4} = \frac{20}{4} = 5.0 \implies \bar{\mathbf{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2.5 \\ 5.0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Step 2: Compute Centered Deviation Vectors: $$\mathbf{x}_1 - \bar{\mathbf{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} -1.5 \\ -3.0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{x}_2 - \bar{\mathbf{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.5 \\ -1.0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{x}_3 - \bar{\mathbf{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 \\ 0.0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{x}_4 - \bar{\mathbf{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.5 \\ 4.0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Step 3: Compute Covariance Entries with $N-1 = 3$: $$s_{11} = \text{Var}(X_1) = \frac{(-1.5)^2 + (-0.5)^2 + (0.5)^2 + (1.5)^2}{3} = \frac{2.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 2.25}{3} = \frac{5.0}{3} \approx 1.6667$$ $$s_{22} = \text{Var}(X_2) = \frac{(-3.0)^2 + (-1.0)^2 + (0.0)^2 + (4.0)^2}{3} = \frac{9.0 + 1.0 + 0.0 + 16.0}{3} = \frac{26.0}{3} \approx 8.6667$$ $$s_{12} = \text{Cov}(X_1, X_2) = \frac{(-1.5)(-3.0) + (-0.5)(-1.0) + (0.5)(0.0) + (1.5)(4.0)}{3} = \frac{4.5 + 0.5 + 0.0 + 6.0}{3} = \frac{11.0}{3} \approx 3.6667$$ $$\mathbf{S} = \begin{bmatrix} 5/3 & 11/3 \\ 11/3 & 26/3 \end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 1.6667 & 3.6667 \\ 3.6667 & 8.6667 \end{bmatrix}$$

Step 4: Compute Pearson Correlation Coefficient: $$r_{12} = \frac{s_{12}}{\sqrt{s_{11} s_{22}}} = \frac{11/3}{\sqrt{(5/3)(26/3)}} = \frac{11}{\sqrt{130}} = \frac{11}{11.40175} \approx 0.9648$$ Result: $r_{12} \approx 0.965$, demonstrating an extremely strong positive linear relationship between the two features.

Problem 1.3.3 Medium Meta Netflix

QUESTION: An ad-serving platform logs impressions and clicks. Model the user click action as a Bernoulli random variable $Y \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p)$ where $P(Y=1) = p$ and $P(Y=0) = 1-p$. Given $N$ i.i.d. observations containing $k$ clicks and $N-k$ non-clicks:

  1. Derive the log-likelihood function $\ell(p)$ and the score function $S(p) = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial p}$.
  2. Solve for the Maximum Likelihood Estimator $\hat{p}_{\text{MLE}}$ and prove strict concavity via the second derivative $\frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial p^2}$.
  3. For a new campaign with $k=42$ clicks out of $N=500$ impressions, compute $\hat{p}_{\text{MLE}}$ and construct the $95\%$ Wald Confidence Interval $\hat{p} \pm 1.96 \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{N}}$.
✓ Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical & Numerical Solution

Step 1: Formulate Likelihood and Log-Likelihood:
For $N$ independent observations $y_1, \dots, y_N \in \{0, 1\}$ with $k = \sum_{i=1}^N y_i$: $$L(p) = \prod_{i=1}^N p^{y_i} (1-p)^{1 - y_i} = p^k (1-p)^{N-k}$$ Take the natural logarithm to obtain the log-likelihood: $$\ell(p) = \ln L(p) = k \ln(p) + (N-k) \ln(1-p)$$ Differentiate with respect to $p$ to find the score function: $$S(p) = \frac{d\ell}{dp} = \frac{k}{p} - \frac{N-k}{1-p}$$

Step 2: Solve for $\hat{p}_{\text{MLE}}$ and Verify Global Maximum:
Set score to zero: $$\frac{k}{p} - \frac{N-k}{1-p} = 0 \implies k(1-p) = p(N-k) \implies k - kp = Np - kp \implies Np = k$$ $$\hat{p}_{\text{MLE}} = \frac{k}{N}$$ Check second derivative: $$\frac{d^2\ell}{dp^2} = -\frac{k}{p^2} - \frac{N-k}{(1-p)^2}$$ Since $p \in (0, 1)$, $k \ge 0$, and $N-k \ge 0$, both denominators are positive. Provided $0 < k < N$, $\frac{d^2\ell}{dp^2} < 0$ strictly for all $p \in (0, 1)$. The log-likelihood is strictly concave everywhere, guaranteeing that $\hat{p}_{\text{MLE}} = \frac{k}{N}$ is the unique global maximum.

Step 3: Numerical Computation & Confidence Interval:
For $k=42$ and $N=500$: $$\hat{p}_{\text{MLE}} = \frac{42}{500} = 0.084 \quad (8.40\% \text{ CTR})$$ Compute Standard Error (SE): $$\text{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{N}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.084 \times 0.916}{500}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.076944}{500}} = \sqrt{0.000153888} \approx 0.012405$$ Margin of error at $95\%$ confidence ($z^* = 1.96$): $$\text{ME} = 1.96 \times 0.012405 \approx 0.02431$$ Confidence Interval: $$\text{CI}_{95\%} = [0.084 - 0.02431, \ 0.084 + 0.02431] = [0.05969, \ 0.10831] \approx [5.97\%, \ 10.83\%]$$ Result: The MLE CTR is $8.40\%$, and we are $95\%$ confident the true conversion rate lies between $5.97\%$ and $10.83\%$.

✓ Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical & Algorithmic Trace

Step 1: Conjugacy Proof:

Problem 1.3.5 Hard Google DeepMind Meta FAIR

QUESTION: Prove rigorously from first principles that Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) estimation of a linear regression model with Gaussian observation noise $y_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{w} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x}_i, \sigma^2)$ and an isotropic zero-mean Gaussian prior over weights $\mathbf{w} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma_w^2 \mathbf{I})$ produces the exact Ridge Regression ($L_2$ Regularization) closed-form solution:

$$\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\text{MAP}} = (\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{y}$$

Explicitly derive the exact formula for the regularization hyperparameter $\lambda$ in terms of the noise variance $\sigma^2$ and prior variance $\sigma_w^2$.

✓ Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proof

Step 1: Formulate the Posterior via Bayes' Theorem:
$$P(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathbf{X}, \mathbf{y}) = \frac{P(\mathbf{y} \mid \mathbf{X}, \mathbf{w}) P(\mathbf{w})}{P(\mathbf{y} \mid \mathbf{X})}$$ Taking the negative logarithm (and discarding $P(\mathbf{y} \mid \mathbf{X})$ which does not depend on $\mathbf{w}$): $$-\ln P(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathbf{X}, \mathbf{y}) = -\ln P(\mathbf{y} \mid \mathbf{X}, \mathbf{w}) - \ln P(\mathbf{w}) + \text{const}$$

Step 2: Expand Likelihood and Prior:
Under i.i.d. Gaussian noise with variance $\sigma^2$, the likelihood over $N$ observations in matrix form is: $$P(\mathbf{y} \mid \mathbf{X}, \mathbf{w}) = \left(\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2}\right)^{N/2} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}\|_2^2 \right)$$ $$-\ln P(\mathbf{y} \mid \mathbf{X}, \mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w})^T (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}) + \frac{N}{2} \ln(2\pi\sigma^2)$$ The prior distribution over $d$-dimensional weight vector $\mathbf{w}$ is $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma_w^2 \mathbf{I})$: $$P(\mathbf{w}) = \left(\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_w^2}\right)^{d/2} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2\sigma_w^2} \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{w} \right)$$ $$-\ln P(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{2\sigma_w^2} \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{w} + \frac{d}{2} \ln(2\pi\sigma_w^2)$$

Step 3: Combine and Minimize the Objective:
$$\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\min_{\mathbf{w}} \left[ \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w})^T (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}) + \frac{1}{2\sigma_w^2} \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{w} \right]$$ Multiply the entire expression by $2\sigma^2$: $$\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\min_{\mathbf{w}} \left[ (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w})^T (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}) + \frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma_w^2} \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{w} \right]$$ Define $\lambda = \frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma_w^2}$. The objective function becomes: $$J(\mathbf{w}) = (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w})^T (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}) + \lambda \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{w}$$ This is the exact definition of the Ridge Regression objective function!

Step 4: Differentiate and Solve for $\mathbf{w}$:
Expand $J(\mathbf{w})$: $$J(\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{y}^T \mathbf{y} - 2 \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{y} + \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X} \mathbf{w} + \lambda \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{w}$$ Compute gradient with respect to $\mathbf{w}$ using matrix calculus: $$\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} J(\mathbf{w}) = -2 \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{y} + 2 \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X} \mathbf{w} + 2 \lambda \mathbf{w}$$ Set gradient to zero: $$\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X} \mathbf{w} + \lambda \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{y} \implies (\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I}) \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{y}$$ Because $\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X}$ is positive semi-definite and $\lambda > 0$, $(\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I})$ is strictly positive definite and always invertible: $$\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\text{MAP}} = (\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{y}, \quad \text{with } \lambda = \frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma_w^2}$$ Q.E.D. $L_2$ regularization is the direct analytical consequence of a Gaussian prior, where the regularizer $\lambda$ represents the ratio of measurement noise variance to prior weight variance!

Problem 1.3.6 Hard OpenAI Anthropic

QUESTION: In Gaussian Process Regression, Kalman Filtering, and Generative Diffusion Models, we partition a joint Gaussian random vector into observed variables $\mathbf{x}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^p$ and unobserved variables $\mathbf{x}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^q$:

$$\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{x}_1 \\ \mathbf{x}_2 \end{bmatrix} \sim \mathcal{N}\left( \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{\mu}_1 \\ \boldsymbol{\mu}_2 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11} & \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12} \\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21} & \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22} \end{bmatrix} \right)$$

Prove that the conditional distribution $P(\mathbf{x}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}_2)$ is also a Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1|2}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1|2})$, and derive the closed-form expressions for the conditional mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1|2}$ and conditional covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1|2}$.

✓ Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proof

Step 1: Define Linear Transformation of Uncorrelated Variables:
We construct a transformation that decorrelates $\mathbf{x}_1$ from $\mathbf{x}_2$. Let: $$\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{A} \mathbf{x}_2$$ where $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{q \times p}$ is a constant matrix chosen such that $\text{Cov}(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{x}_2) = \mathbf{0}$.
Compute the cross-covariance: $$\text{Cov}(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{x}_2) = \text{Cov}(\mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{A} \mathbf{x}_2, \mathbf{x}_2) = \text{Cov}(\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2) - \mathbf{A} \text{Cov}(\mathbf{x}_2, \mathbf{x}_2) = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12} - \mathbf{A} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}$$ Setting this to $\mathbf{0}$ yields: $$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12} - \mathbf{A} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22} = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{A} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}$$ Therefore, the variable $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{x}_1 - \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1} \mathbf{x}_2$ has zero covariance with $\mathbf{x}_2$.

Step 2: Exploit Independence of Jointly Gaussian Variables:
Because $\mathbf{x}_1$ and $\mathbf{x}_2$ are jointly Gaussian, $\mathbf{z}$ and $\mathbf{x}_2$ are also jointly Gaussian. For jointly Gaussian random vectors, zero covariance implies complete statistical independence: $$\mathbf{z} \perp \mathbf{x}_2$$ Therefore, the conditional distribution of $\mathbf{z}$ given $\mathbf{x}_2$ is identical to its unconditional marginal distribution: $$P(\mathbf{z} \mid \mathbf{x}_2) = P(\mathbf{z})$$

Step 3: Compute Mean and Covariance of $\mathbf{z}$: $$\boldsymbol{\mu}_z = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_1] - \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1} \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_2] = \boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\mu}_2$$ $$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_z = \text{Var}(\mathbf{z}) = \text{Var}(\mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_2) = \text{Var}(\mathbf{x}_1) + \mathbf{A} \text{Var}(\mathbf{x}_2) \mathbf{A}^T - \mathbf{A} \text{Cov}(\mathbf{x}_2, \mathbf{x}_1) - \text{Cov}(\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2) \mathbf{A}^T$$ Substitute $\mathbf{A} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}$ and note $\text{Cov}(\mathbf{x}_2, \mathbf{x}_1) = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}^T$: $$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_z = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11} + (\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1}) \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22} (\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}) - 2 \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}$$ $$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_z = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11} + \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21} - 2 \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11} - \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}$$

Step 4: Recover the Conditional Distribution of $\mathbf{x}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}_2$:
Since $\mathbf{x}_1 = \mathbf{z} + \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1} \mathbf{x}_2$, and $\mathbf{x}_2$ is treated as a fixed constant condition: $$\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}_2] = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z} \mid \mathbf{x}_2] + \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1} \mathbf{x}_2 = \boldsymbol{\mu}_z + \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1} \mathbf{x}_2$$ $$\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1|2} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_1 + \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1} (\mathbf{x}_2 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)$$ $$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1|2} = \text{Var}(\mathbf{x}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}_2) = \text{Var}(\mathbf{z} \mid \mathbf{x}_2) = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_z = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11} - \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}$$ Q.E.D. The conditional distribution of a multivariate Gaussian is Gaussian, with mean shifted linearly by the residual $(\mathbf{x}_2 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)$ and covariance reduced by the Schur complement $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}^{-1} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}$!

Problem 1.3.7 Medium Google

QUESTION: An AI research scientist estimates the noise variance $\sigma^2$ of sensor inputs using sample variance $\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar{x})^2$. Which of the following statements is strictly mathematically TRUE regarding this estimator?

  1. $\hat{\sigma}^2$ is an unbiased estimator for all sample sizes $N \ge 2$, but its variance increases as $N \to \infty$.
  2. $\hat{\sigma}^2$ is a biased estimator for finite $N$, but it is asymptotically unbiased and consistent as $N \to \infty$.
  3. $\hat{\sigma}^2$ is biased because $\bar{x}$ is a biased estimator of the true population mean $\mu$.
  4. Replacing $\frac{1}{N}$ with $\frac{1}{N-1}$ eliminates both the bias and the variance of the estimator.
✓ Complete Conceptual Analysis & Explanation

Correct Option: B

Exhaustive Analysis of All Options:

  • Option B is TRUE: As proven in Section 6, $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\sigma}^2] = \frac{N-1}{N} \sigma^2 \neq \sigma^2$, making it strictly biased for any finite $N$. However, $\lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{N-1}{N} \sigma^2 = \sigma^2$, so its bias vanishes asymptotically ($\lim_{N \to \infty} \text{Bias} = 0$). Furthermore, by the Weak Law of Large Numbers, $\text{Var}(\hat{\sigma}^2) \to 0$ as $N \to \infty$, which proves that $\hat{\sigma}^2 \overset{P}{\to} \sigma^2$ (it is a statistically consistent estimator).
  • Option A is FALSE: $\hat{\sigma}^2$ is not unbiased for finite $N$; it has a systematic negative bias. Furthermore, its variance decreases as $\mathcal{O}(1/N)$, never increases.
  • Option C is FALSE: The sample mean $\bar{x}$ is perfectly unbiased ($\mathbb{E}[\bar{x}] = \mu$). The source of bias in $\hat{\sigma}^2$ is that squared deviations are computed around $\bar{x}$ (which minimizes the sum of squared deviations for that specific sample) rather than $\mu$, systematically underestimating true variance.
  • Option D is FALSE: Bessel's correction $\frac{1}{N-1}$ eliminates the bias, but it does NOT eliminate the variance of the estimator. Any finite sample estimator has strictly positive variance.

Problem 1.3.8 Hard DeepMind

QUESTION: Consider the following Assertion (A) and Reason (R):

Assertion (A): Among all continuous probability distributions supported on $\mathbb{R}^d$ with a specified mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and fixed covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$, the Multivariate Normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})$ uniquely maximizes differential Shannon entropy.

Reason (R): When training a neural network with weight decay ($L_2$ penalty), the optimizer implicitly places an uninformative Jeffreys prior over the model weight parameters.

  1. Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
  2. Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).
  3. (A) is true, but (R) is false.
  4. (A) is false, but (R) is true.
✓ Complete Conceptual Analysis & Explanation

Correct Option: C

Exhaustive Analysis of Assertion & Reason:

  • Assertion (A) is TRUE: By the Principle of Maximum Entropy (Jaynes), given only constraints on the first and second moments ($\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}] = \boldsymbol{\mu}$ and $\mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^T] = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}$), the Gaussian distribution makes the fewest unverified assumptions and maximizes differential entropy $H(p) = \frac{1}{2} \ln((2\pi e)^d |\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|)$. Any other distribution with the same covariance contains more structure/constraints and thus less entropy. This is the theoretical foundation for why Gaussian noise is the default model in diffusion and physics.
  • Reason (R) is FALSE: As proven in Problem 1.3.5, an $L_2$ weight decay penalty corresponds to an informative zero-mean Gaussian prior $P(\mathbf{w}) \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma_w^2 \mathbf{I})$. A Jeffreys prior is an invariant uninformative prior proportional to the square root of the Fisher Information determinant ($P(\theta) \propto \sqrt{\det \mathcal{I}(\theta)}$), which for a location parameter is uniform/flat ($P(w) \propto 1$), resulting in zero regularization penalty.
  • Therefore, Assertion (A) is true, but Reason (R) is completely false.

import numpy as np

def simulate_bayesian_coin(true_bias=0.7, n_flips=1000):
    """
    Demonstrates Beta-Binomial conjugate Bayesian belief updating.
    Prior: Beta(alpha=1, beta=1) (Uniform prior)
    """
    np.random.seed(42)
    flips = np.random.binomial(n=1, p=true_bias, size=n_flips)

    alpha_prior, beta_prior = 1.0, 1.0 # Prior: completely uninformative
    
    # Observe flips sequentially
    heads = np.sum(flips)
    tails = n_flips - heads

    alpha_posterior = alpha_prior + heads
    beta_posterior = beta_prior + tails

    # Posterior Mean Estimate: E[theta] = alpha / (alpha + beta)
    estimated_bias = alpha_posterior / (alpha_posterior + beta_posterior)
    mle_bias = heads / n_flips

    print(f"True Probability   : {true_bias:.4f}")
    print(f"Observed Flips     : {n_flips} ({heads} Heads, {tails} Tails)")
    print(f"MLE Estimate       : {mle_bias:.4f}")
    print(f"Bayesian Posterior : {estimated_bias:.4f}")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    simulate_bayesian_coin(true_bias=0.7, n_flips=5000)
