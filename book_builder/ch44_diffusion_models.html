1. Why Diffusion Succeeded Where GANs Failed

For nearly a decade, Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) represented the state of the art in generative vision. However, GANs suffered from notorious engineering pathologies: the two-player minimax game ($\min_G \max_D$) frequently diverged into limit cycles, suffered from catastrophic Mode Collapse (the generator producing a narrow set of repetitive outputs), and lacked any meaningful likelihood metric.

In 2020, Jonathan Ho, Ajay Jain, and Pieter Abbeel published Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM), grounding image synthesis in non-equilibrium thermodynamics (Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015). Instead of an unstable adversarial duel, Diffusion transforms image generation into a stable, convex Mean Squared Error (MSE) denoising regression task with zero mode collapse and unprecedented sample coverage.

2. The Forward and Reverse Markov Chains

2.1 The Forward (Noising) Process

Given an initial pristine data sample $\mathbf{x}_0 \sim q(\mathbf{x}_0)$, the forward diffusion process gradually destroys spatial structure by adding Gaussian noise over $T$ discrete timesteps (typically $T = 1,000$) according to a variance schedule $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_T \in (0, 1)$:

$$q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\left( \mathbf{x}_t; \sqrt{1 - \beta_t} \mathbf{x}_{t-1}, \beta_t \mathbf{I} \right)$$

As $t \to T$, the cumulative noise overwhelms the signal, such that $\mathbf{x}_T$ becomes statistically indistinguishable from pure isotropic white noise: $\mathbf{x}_T \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$.

🏆 The Closed-Form Forward Marginal Shortcut

Simulating 1,000 intermediate Markov transitions to sample $\mathbf{x}_t$ would destroy training throughput. Let $\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$ and define the cumulative variance product:

$$\bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{s=1}^t \alpha_s = \prod_{s=1}^t (1 - \beta_s)$$

Using the reproductive property of independent Gaussian random variables, the marginal distribution of $\mathbf{x}_t$ conditioned directly on $\mathbf{x}_0$ is derived in closed form:

$$q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0) = \mathcal{N}\left( \mathbf{x}_t; \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_0, (1 - \bar{\alpha}_t) \mathbf{I} \right)$$ $$\implies \mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \quad \text{where } \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$$

Any arbitrary noisy state $\mathbf{x}_t$ can be generated in a single float operation without evaluating intermediate timesteps!

2.2 The Reverse (Denoising) Process & U-Net Training

The true reverse distribution $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t)$ requires integrating over the entire data distribution $q(\mathbf{x}_0)$, which is intractable. However, when conditioned on the initial image $\mathbf{x}_0$, Bayes' rule proves that the reverse conditional posterior is an exact Gaussian distribution:

$$q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0) = \mathcal{N}\left( \mathbf{x}_{t-1}; \tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0), \tilde{\beta}_t \mathbf{I} \right)$$

where the posterior parameters are:

$$\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0) = \frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} \beta_t}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_0 + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_t$$ $$\tilde{\beta}_t = \frac{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \beta_t$$

By expressing $\mathbf{x}_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}} \left( \mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \right)$, the posterior mean simplifies to:

$$\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} \left( \mathbf{x}_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}} \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \right)$$

Thus, rather than predicting the clean image $\mathbf{x}_0$ directly, we parameterize a time-conditioned U-Net $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)$ to predict the exact noise vector $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ that was injected into $\mathbf{x}_0$. Ho et al. showed that optimizing the simplified $L_2$ regression loss yields state-of-the-art sample quality:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{simple}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathcal{U}(1, T), \mathbf{x}_0, \boldsymbol{\epsilon}} \left[ \left\| \boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) \right\|_2^2 \right]$$

3. Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG): Ho & Salimans, 2021

To steer diffusion toward a textual conditioning prompt $c$ (e.g. "cyberpunk city at dusk, 8k"), early systems used an auxiliary image classifier $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t} \ln p(c \mid \mathbf{x}_t)$. However, training noisy image classifiers is brittle and computationally expensive.

Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) trains a single U-Net on both conditional text embeddings $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, c)$ and unconditional empty tokens $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \emptyset)$ by randomly dropping the text conditioning ($c = \emptyset$) for $10\%$ of training batches.

During inference, the final noise estimate is extrapolated along the vector difference with guidance scale $s \ge 1$:

$$\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, c) = \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \emptyset) + s \cdot \left( \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, c) - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \emptyset) \right)$$

Implicitly, by Bayes' rule, CFG shifts the sampling score in the direction of the implicit classifier gradient:

$$\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t} \ln p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid c) = \nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t} \ln p(\mathbf{x}_t) + s \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t} \ln p(c \mid \mathbf{x}_t)$$
  • $s = 1.0$: Standard conditional generation (high diversity, modest prompt adherence).
  • $s \in [7.0, 9.0]$: The production sweet spot (striking visual fidelity, sharp prompt adherence).
  • $s > 15.0$: Severe over-saturation, color burn, and distorted geometric artifacts.

4. Latent Diffusion Models (Stable Diffusion): Rombach et al., 2022

Executing diffusion in pixel space on a $512 \times 512 \times 3$ image requires calculating self-attention and convolutions across $786,432$ float coordinates at every step, creating enormous compute barriers. Robin Rombach et al. solved this with Latent Diffusion Models (LDM):

  1. Perceptual Compression: A pre-trained VAE encoder compresses the $512 \times 512 \times 3$ pixel image into a compact latent representation $\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{64 \times 64 \times 4}$ ($16,384$ coordinates).
  2. Latent Diffusion: The forward noise schedule and reverse denoising U-Net run exclusively inside the compressed $64 \times 64$ latent manifold, slashing FLOP requirements by $48\times$!
  3. Cross-Attention Conditioning: Text tokens from a pre-trained text encoder (CLIP ViT-L/14 or T5) are injected into the U-Net through multi-head cross-attention blocks: $$\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{W}_Q \mathbf{z}_t, \quad \mathbf{K} = \mathbf{W}_K \mathbf{c}_{\text{text}}, \quad \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{W}_V \mathbf{c}_{\text{text}}$$
  4. High-Resolution Decoding: Once the latent representation $\mathbf{z}_0$ is completely denoised, the VAE decoder expands it back into the full $512 \times 512$ RGB pixel image in a single forward pass.

Problem 4.4.1 Hard Google OpenAI

QUESTION (Closed-Form Forward Diffusion Theorem Proof): In DDPM, the single-step forward transition is defined as:

$$\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{1 - \beta_t} \mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-1}, \quad \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-1} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$$

Let $\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$ and $\bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{s=1}^t \alpha_s$. Prove by mathematical induction that for any arbitrary timestep $t$, $\mathbf{x}_t$ can be sampled directly from $\mathbf{x}_0$ via the closed-form equation:

$$\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \quad \text{where } \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$$
✓ Complete First-Principles Mathematical Proof

Step 1: Base Case ($t=1$): For $t=1$, $\bar{\alpha}_1 = \alpha_1 = 1 - \beta_1$. The single-step transition gives: $$\mathbf{x}_1 = \sqrt{\alpha_1} \mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_1} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_0 = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_1} \mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_1} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_0$$ The base case holds trivially.

Step 2: Induction Hypothesis: Assume the proposition holds for timestep $t-1$: $$\mathbf{x}_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} \mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-2}^*, \quad \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-2}^* \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$$

Step 3: Inductive Step for Timestep $t$: From the single-step Markov definition: $$\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t} \mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_t} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-1}, \quad \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-1} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$$ Substitute the induction hypothesis for $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$: $$\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t} \left( \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} \mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-2}^* \right) + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_t} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-1}$$ $$\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t \bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} \mathbf{x}_0 + \left( \sqrt{\alpha_t(1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1})} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-2}^* + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_t} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-1} \right)$$ Since $\bar{\alpha}_t = \alpha_t \bar{\alpha}_{t-1}$, the first term is $\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_0$. Now consider the noise term $a \mathbf{X} + b \mathbf{Y}$ with $\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{Y} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ independent: $$a = \sqrt{\alpha_t(1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}, \quad b = \sqrt{1 - \alpha_t}$$ The sum of two independent Gaussians is a Gaussian with variance $a^2 + b^2$: $$\sigma_{\text{combined}}^2 = a^2 + b^2 = \alpha_t(1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1}) + (1 - \alpha_t) = \alpha_t - \alpha_t \bar{\alpha}_{t-1} + 1 - \alpha_t = 1 - \alpha_t \bar{\alpha}_{t-1} = 1 - \bar{\alpha}_t$$ Therefore: $$\sqrt{\alpha_t(1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1})} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-2}^* + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_t} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-1} = \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}$$ where $\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$. Substituting this back: $$\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \quad \blacksquare$$ By mathematical induction, the closed-form marginal holds for all $t \in \{1, \dots, T\}$.

Problem 4.4.2 Medium Meta Apple

QUESTION (Forward Diffusion Alpha-Bar Numerical Drill): A toy diffusion model operating on 2D coordinates has clean sample $\mathbf{x}_0 = [1.0, -0.5]^T$. The variance schedule for the first two steps is $\beta_1 = 0.10, \beta_2 = 0.20$. The sampled standard Gaussian noise vectors are $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_1 = [0.20, -0.40]^T$ (at $t=1$) and $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_2 = [-0.10, 0.30]^T$ (at $t=2$).

  1. Calculate $\alpha_1, \alpha_2$, and cumulative products $\bar{\alpha}_1, \bar{\alpha}_2$.
  2. Calculate $\mathbf{x}_1$ using the step-by-step Markov transition $\mathbf{x}_1 = \sqrt{\alpha_1} \mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_1} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_1$.
  3. Calculate $\mathbf{x}_2$ using the step-by-step Markov transition $\mathbf{x}_2 = \sqrt{\alpha_2} \mathbf{x}_1 + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_2} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_2$.
  4. Construct the single equivalent Gaussian noise vector $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^*$ such that the direct shortcut $\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_2} \mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_2} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}^*$ matches $\mathbf{x}_2$ identically.
✓ Step-by-Step Numerical Verification

Step 1: Noise Schedule Parameters:

  • $\alpha_1 = 1 - \beta_1 = 1 - 0.10 = \mathbf{0.90}$
  • $\alpha_2 = 1 - \beta_2 = 1 - 0.20 = \mathbf{0.80}$
  • $\bar{\alpha}_1 = \alpha_1 = \mathbf{0.90}$
  • $\bar{\alpha}_2 = \alpha_1 \times \alpha_2 = 0.90 \times 0.80 = \mathbf{0.72}$
Square roots: $$\sqrt{\alpha_1} = \sqrt{0.90} \approx 0.94868, \quad \sqrt{1 - \alpha_1} = \sqrt{0.10} \approx 0.31623$$ $$\sqrt{\alpha_2} = \sqrt{0.80} \approx 0.89443, \quad \sqrt{1 - \alpha_2} = \sqrt{0.20} \approx 0.44721$$ $$\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_2} = \sqrt{0.72} \approx 0.84853, \quad \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_2} = \sqrt{0.28} \approx 0.52915$$

Step 2: Step-by-Step Transition to $\mathbf{x}_1$: $$\mathbf{x}_1 = \sqrt{0.90} \begin{bmatrix} 1.0 \\ -0.5 \end{bmatrix} + \sqrt{0.10} \begin{bmatrix} 0.20 \\ -0.40 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.94868 + 0.31623(0.20) \\ -0.47434 + 0.31623(-0.40) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.94868 + 0.06325 \\ -0.47434 - 0.12649 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{\begin{bmatrix} 1.01193 \\ -0.60083 \end{bmatrix}}$$

Step 3: Step-by-Step Transition to $\mathbf{x}_2$: $$\mathbf{x}_2 = \sqrt{0.80} \mathbf{x}_1 + \sqrt{0.20} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_2 = 0.89443 \begin{bmatrix} 1.01193 \\ -0.60083 \end{bmatrix} + 0.44721 \begin{bmatrix} -0.10 \\ 0.30 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.90510 - 0.04472 \\ -0.53740 + 0.13416 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{\begin{bmatrix} 0.86038 \\ -0.40324 \end{bmatrix}}$$

Step 4: Direct Shortcut Verification: We equate $\mathbf{x}_2 = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_2} \mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_2} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}^*$: $$\begin{bmatrix} 0.86038 \\ -0.40324 \end{bmatrix} = 0.84853 \begin{bmatrix} 1.0 \\ -0.5 \end{bmatrix} + 0.52915 \boldsymbol{\epsilon}^* = \begin{bmatrix} 0.84853 \\ -0.42426 \end{bmatrix} + 0.52915 \boldsymbol{\epsilon}^*$$ Solving for $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^*$: $$\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^* = \frac{1}{0.52915} \left( \begin{bmatrix} 0.86038 \\ -0.40324 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 0.84853 \\ -0.42426 \end{bmatrix} \right) = \frac{1}{0.52915} \begin{bmatrix} 0.01185 \\ 0.02102 \end{bmatrix} \approx \mathbf{\begin{bmatrix} 0.0224 \\ 0.0397 \end{bmatrix}}$$ This proves that the composite multi-step transition matches the single direct Gaussian step $\mathbf{x}_2 = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_2}\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_2}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^*$ identically.

Problem 4.4.3 Hard Stability AI Runway

QUESTION (Classifier-Free Guidance Extrapolation Trace): During a reverse diffusion step, the U-Net predicts two 2D noise vectors for a noisy latent coordinate:

  • Unconditional noise prediction: $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, \emptyset) = [0.12, -0.08]^T$
  • Text-conditioned noise prediction: $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, c) = [0.44, -0.32]^T$
  1. Compute the guided noise prediction $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_\theta$ for guidance scales $s = 1.0$ (standard conditional), $s = 7.5$ (production default), and $s = 15.0$ (over-saturated).
  2. Calculate the Euclidean norm $\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_\theta\|_2$ across all three guidance scales.
  3. Explain why excessively large guidance scales ($s > 15$) trigger the visual artifact known as Latent Clamping / Color Burn, and how Dynamic Thresholding (Imagen) resolves it.
✓ Step-by-Step CFG Extrapolation Trace

Step 1: Guided Noise Vector Calculations: Formula: $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_\theta = \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\emptyset + s (\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_c - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\emptyset)$. Difference vector: $$\boldsymbol{\Delta} = \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_c - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\emptyset = \begin{bmatrix} 0.44 - 0.12 \\ -0.32 - (-0.08) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.32 \\ -0.24 \end{bmatrix}$$

  • For $s = 1.0$: $$\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{1.0} = \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\emptyset + 1.0 \boldsymbol{\Delta} = \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_c = \mathbf{\begin{bmatrix} 0.44 \\ -0.32 \end{bmatrix}}$$
  • For $s = 7.5$: $$\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{7.5} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.12 \\ -0.08 \end{bmatrix} + 7.5 \begin{bmatrix} 0.32 \\ -0.24 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.12 + 2.40 \\ -0.08 - 1.80 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{\begin{bmatrix} 2.52 \\ -1.88 \end{bmatrix}}$$
  • For $s = 15.0$: $$\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{15.0} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.12 \\ -0.08 \end{bmatrix} + 15.0 \begin{bmatrix} 0.32 \\ -0.24 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.12 + 4.80 \\ -0.08 - 3.60 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{\begin{bmatrix} 4.92 \\ -3.68 \end{bmatrix}}$$

Step 2: Vector Norm Growth:

  • $\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{1.0}\|_2 = \sqrt{0.44^2 + (-0.32)^2} = \sqrt{0.1936 + 0.1024} = \sqrt{0.296} \approx \mathbf{0.544}$
  • $\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{7.5}\|_2 = \sqrt{2.52^2 + (-1.88)^2} = \sqrt{6.3504 + 3.5344} = \sqrt{9.8848} \approx \mathbf{3.144}$
  • $\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{15.0}\|_2 = \sqrt{4.92^2 + (-3.68)^2} = \sqrt{24.2064 + 13.5424} = \sqrt{37.7488} \approx \mathbf{6.144}$

Step 3: Latent Clamping Pathology & Resolution: When $s > 15$, the predicted noise norm explodes ($6.14 \gg 1.0$). In the reverse step: $$\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 = \frac{\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}}$$ An excessively large $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}$ drives the predicted clean latent coordinates $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$ far outside the standard Gaussian support (e.g. coordinates reach $\pm 5.0$ instead of $[-1, 1]$). When these extreme values are converted to pixel space by the VAE decoder and clamped to $[0, 255]$, color channels saturate to pure neon cyan, yellow, or blown-out white (color burn). Dynamic Thresholding (Saharia et al., Imagen): Instead of clipping latents to a fixed $[-1, 1]$, compute the $99.5$-th percentile absolute value $s_{\text{thresh}} = \text{percentile}(|\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0|, 99.5)$. If $s_{\text{thresh}} > 1.0$, divide the entire latent map by $s_{\text{thresh}}$: $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 \leftarrow \hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 / s_{\text{thresh}}$, preserving relative color contrasts without clipping artifacts.

Problem 4.4.4 Hard Midjourney ByteDance

QUESTION (DDIM Deterministic Inversion & ODE Step Trace): Standard DDPM sampling requires $T = 1,000$ sequential stochastic evaluations. Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models (DDIM; Song et al., 2020) generalize DDPM to a non-Markovian deterministic ODE with sampling parameter $\sigma_t = 0$:

$$\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{i-1}} = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{\tau_{i-1}}} \underbrace{\left( \frac{\mathbf{x}_{\tau_i} - \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{\tau_i}} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{\tau_i})}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{\tau_i}}} \right)}_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0} + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{\tau_{i-1}}} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{\tau_i})$$

Suppose an accelerated scheduler executes an inference trajectory skipping from timestep $\tau_i = 600$ to $\tau_{i-1} = 550$. The parameters are:

$$\bar{\alpha}_{600} = 0.36 \implies \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{600}} = 0.60, \quad \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{600}} = 0.80$$ $$\bar{\alpha}_{550} = 0.49 \implies \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{550}} = 0.70, \quad \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{550}} = \sqrt{0.51} \approx 0.71414$$

Given current noisy state $\mathbf{x}_{600} = [1.40, -0.60]^T$ and U-Net noise prediction $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta = [1.00, -0.50]^T$:

  1. Calculate the predicted clean sample $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$.
  2. Calculate the updated state $\mathbf{x}_{550}$.
  3. Explain why setting $\sigma_t = 0$ allows exact deterministic image inversion ($\mathbf{x}_0 \to \mathbf{x}_T \to \mathbf{x}_0$) for real image editing.
✓ Step-by-Step DDIM Trajectory Computation

Step 1: Clean Sample Prediction $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$: $$\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 = \frac{\mathbf{x}_{600} - \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{600}} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{600}}} = \frac{\begin{bmatrix} 1.40 \\ -0.60 \end{bmatrix} - 0.80 \begin{bmatrix} 1.00 \\ -0.50 \end{bmatrix}}{0.60} = \frac{\begin{bmatrix} 1.40 - 0.80 \\ -0.60 + 0.40 \end{bmatrix}}{0.60} = \frac{\begin{bmatrix} 0.60 \\ -0.20 \end{bmatrix}}{0.60} = \mathbf{\begin{bmatrix} 1.0000 \\ -0.3333 \end{bmatrix}}$$

Step 2: DDIM Step to $\tau_{i-1} = 550$: $$\mathbf{x}_{550} = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{550}} \hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{550}} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta$$ $$\mathbf{x}_{550} = 0.70 \begin{bmatrix} 1.0000 \\ -0.3333 \end{bmatrix} + 0.71414 \begin{bmatrix} 1.0000 \\ -0.5000 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.7000 \\ -0.2333 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0.71414 \\ -0.35707 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{\begin{bmatrix} 1.4141 \\ -0.5904 \end{bmatrix}}$$

Step 3: Deterministic Inversion Mechanics: When $\sigma_t = 0$, the stochastic noise injection term $\sigma_t \boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ vanishes completely. The reverse diffusion process becomes the exact numerical discretization of a deterministic Probability Flow ODE (Song et al., 2020). Because an ODE has no random stochasticity, time can be run backward and forward with zero entropy gain:

  • Forward Inversion ($\mathbf{x}_0 \to \mathbf{x}_T$): Integrating the ODE backward in time maps any real input image $\mathbf{x}_0$ to its unique, deterministic latent noise representation $\mathbf{x}_T$.
  • Image Editing: Once inverted, an engineer modifies the text prompt $c_{\text{new}}$ (e.g. adding "wearing sunglasses") and runs the reverse ODE from $\mathbf{x}_T$ to synthesize the edited image while perfectly preserving non-target facial identity!

Problem 4.4.5 Hard OpenAI DeepMind

QUESTION (Mathematical Proof of the Gaussian Reverse Conditional Posterior): In DDPM, the reverse step is conditioned on the clean data sample $\mathbf{x}_0$. Applying Bayes' Rule:

$$q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0) = q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_0) \frac{q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}{q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}$$
  1. Prove that $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0)$ is an exact Gaussian distribution $\mathcal{N}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t, \tilde{\beta}_t \mathbf{I})$.
  2. Derive the analytical formulas for the posterior mean $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t$ and posterior variance $\tilde{\beta}_t$: $$\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t = \frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\beta_t}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_0 + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_t, \quad \tilde{\beta}_t = \frac{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \beta_t$$
✓ Complete First-Principles Mathematical Proof

Step 1: Express Probability Densities in Exponential Form: All three terms in Bayes' rule are Gaussians: $$q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1}) \propto \exp\left( -\frac{\|\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{\alpha_t} \mathbf{x}_{t-1}\|^2}{2\beta_t} \right)$$ $$q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_0) \propto \exp\left( -\frac{\|\mathbf{x}_{t-1} - \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} \mathbf{x}_0\|^2}{2(1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1})} \right)$$ $$q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0) \propto \exp\left( -\frac{\|\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_0\|^2}{2(1 - \bar{\alpha}_t)} \right)$$ Multiplying and dividing densities: $$q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0) \propto \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} \left[ \frac{\|\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{\alpha_t} \mathbf{x}_{t-1}\|^2}{\beta_t} + \frac{\|\mathbf{x}_{t-1} - \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} \mathbf{x}_0\|^2}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} - \frac{\|\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_0\|^2}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \right] \right)$$

Step 2: Complete the Square with Respect to $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$: Collect all terms quadratic in $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$: $$\text{Quadratic Term} = \mathbf{x}_{t-1}^T \mathbf{x}_{t-1} \left( \frac{\alpha_t}{\beta_t} + \frac{1}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} \right)$$ Find a common denominator: $$\frac{\alpha_t(1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1}) + \beta_t}{\beta_t(1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1})} = \frac{\alpha_t - \alpha_t \bar{\alpha}_{t-1} + 1 - \alpha_t}{\beta_t(1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1})} = \frac{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}{\beta_t(1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}$$ Since a Gaussian density has quadratic form $\exp(-\frac{1}{2\tilde{\beta}_t} \|\mathbf{x}_{t-1} - \tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t\|^2)$, the coefficient of $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}^2$ is $\frac{1}{\tilde{\beta}_t}$: $$\frac{1}{\tilde{\beta}_t} = \frac{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}{\beta_t(1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1})} \implies \mathbf{\tilde{\beta}_t = \frac{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \beta_t} \quad \blacksquare$$

Step 3: Collect Linear Terms in $\mathbf{x}_{t-1}$: The linear term in the exponent is: $$\text{Linear Term} = 2 \mathbf{x}_{t-1}^T \left( \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}{\beta_t} \mathbf{x}_t + \frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} \mathbf{x}_0 \right)$$ By matching with the expansion of $-\frac{1}{2\tilde{\beta}_t} \|\mathbf{x}_{t-1} - \tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t\|^2 = -\frac{1}{2\tilde{\beta}_t} (\mathbf{x}_{t-1}^2 - 2 \mathbf{x}_{t-1}^T \tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t + \dots)$, the linear coefficient equals $\frac{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t}{\tilde{\beta}_t}$: $$\frac{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t}{\tilde{\beta}_t} = \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}{\beta_t} \mathbf{x}_t + \frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} \mathbf{x}_0$$ Multiplying both sides by $\tilde{\beta}_t = \frac{\beta_t(1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}$: $$\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t = \left(\frac{\beta_t(1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}\right) \left[ \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}{\beta_t} \mathbf{x}_t + \frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} \mathbf{x}_0 \right]$$ Multiplying through: $$\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t = \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_t + \frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} \beta_t}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_0 \quad \blacksquare$$ The posterior distribution is strictly Gaussian, with analytical mean $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t$ and variance $\tilde{\beta}_t$.

QUESTION (Mathematical Equivalence of DDPM and Denoising Score Matching): In Score-Based Generative Modeling (Song & Ermon, 2019), a neural network $\mathbf{s}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)$ is trained to approximate the Stein score of the perturbed data density: $\mathbf{s}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) \approx \nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t} \ln q(\mathbf{x}_t)$.

  1. For the Gaussian forward marginal $q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0) = \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_0, (1 - \bar{\alpha}_t) \mathbf{I})$, calculate the exact analytical score $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t} \ln q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0)$ in terms of injected noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$.
  2. Prove that Ho et al.'s noise prediction model $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)$ and the score matching model $\mathbf{s}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)$ are related by: $$\mathbf{s}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) = -\frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)}{\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}}$$
  3. Prove that the DDPM loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{simple}} = \|\boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\|^2$ is equivalent to Denoising Score Matching weighted by $\sigma_t^2 = 1 - \bar{\alpha}_t$.
✓ Complete Score Matching Equivalence Proof

Step 1: Analytical Score of the Forward Marginal: The Gaussian probability density is: $$q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0) = \frac{1}{(2\pi(1 - \bar{\alpha}_t))^{D/2}} \exp\left( -\frac{\|\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_0\|_2^2}{2(1 - \bar{\alpha}_t)} \right)$$ Taking the natural logarithm: $$\ln q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0) = -\frac{D}{2} \ln(2\pi(1 - \bar{\alpha}_t)) - \frac{\|\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_0\|_2^2}{2(1 - \bar{\alpha}_t)}$$ Differentiating with respect to $\mathbf{x}_t$: $$\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t} \ln q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0) = -\frac{\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_0}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}$$ Using the reparameterization $\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \implies \mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_0 = \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}$: $$\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t} \ln q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0) = -\frac{\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} = \mathbf{-\frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}{\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}}} \quad \blacksquare$$

Step 2: Relationship Between Score and Noise Prediction: Setting the score network to model the true score $\mathbf{s}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) \approx \nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t} \ln q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0)$: $$\mathbf{s}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) = -\frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)}{\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}} \iff \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) = -\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{s}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) \quad \blacksquare$$

Step 3: Loss Function Equivalence: The Denoising Score Matching objective with positive weighting function $\lambda(t)$ is: $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DSM}} = \mathbb{E} \left[ \lambda(t) \left\| \mathbf{s}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) - \nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t} \ln q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0) \right\|_2^2 \right]$$ Substituting $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t} \ln q = -\frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}{\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}}$ and $\mathbf{s}_\theta = -\frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta}{\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}}$: $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DSM}} = \mathbb{E} \left[ \lambda(t) \left\| -\frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta}{\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}} - \left(-\frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}{\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}}\right) \right\|_2^2 \right] = \mathbb{E} \left[ \frac{\lambda(t)}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \left\| \boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) \right\|_2^2 \right]$$ Setting the weight schedule to $\lambda(t) = 1 - \bar{\alpha}_t = \sigma_t^2$: $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DSM}} = \mathbb{E} \left[ \left\| \boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) \right\|_2^2 \right] \equiv \mathcal{L}_{\text{simple}} \quad \blacksquare$$ This proves that DDPM noise prediction is identical to variance-weighted Denoising Score Matching.

QUESTION (The Linear vs. Cosine Variance Schedule Pathology): In their 2021 work "Improved Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models", Nichol & Dhariwal identified that Ho et al.'s original linear variance schedule ($\beta_1 = 10^{-4}$ to $\beta_T = 0.02$) destroys image information too rapidly early in the forward process. Why does the Cosine Schedule:

$$\bar{\alpha}_t = \frac{f(t)}{f(0)}, \quad f(t) = \cos^2\left( \frac{t/T + s}{1 + s} \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} \right)$$

  • (A) The linear schedule causes the GPU to divide by zero at $t=1000$; the cosine schedule replaces division with trigonometry.

Exhaustive Diffusion Analysis:

  • (B) is CORRECT: In the original DDPM linear schedule, $\beta_t$ increases linearly from $10^{-4}$ to $0.02$. Because $\bar{\alpha}_t = \prod (1 - \beta_s)$, $\bar{\alpha}_t$ drops at an accelerating exponential rate. By step $t = 200$ (just $20\%$ of the process), $\bar{\alpha}_t$ has already dropped to $\approx 0.7$, and by $t = 500$, it drops below $0.1$. This destroys high-frequency edge and texture information far too early, leaving the model spending $80\%$ of its capacity on nearly pure Gaussian static. The Cosine Schedule uses a squared cosine function with small offset $s = 0.008$. It begins with a very gentle slope near $t=0$ ($\frac{d\bar{\alpha}}{dt} \approx 0$), preserving subtle textural nuance in early steps, maintains a steady linear drop throughout the middle, and flattens out smoothly at $t=T$, ensuring the network learns multi-scale visual features symmetrically.

  • (C) is INCORRECT: The variance schedule determines the noising trajectory; it does not replace or eliminate the U-Net denoiser.
  • (D) is INCORRECT: $\beta_t$ and $\bar{\alpha}_t$ are scalar parameters, not matrices with eigenvalue spectra.

QUESTION (The VAE Latent Scale Factor in Stable Diffusion): In Stable Diffusion (Rombach et al., 2022), when passing training images through the pre-trained VAE encoder, the output latents $\mathbf{z}$ are multiplied by a magic scaling constant before diffusion training:

  • (B) The pre-trained VAE latent space was regularized with a weak KL penalty ($D_{\text{KL}}$ weight $\approx 10^{-6}$), causing the empirical variance of encoded image latents $\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{z}_{\text{VAE}})$ to be approximately $5.489^2 \approx 30.13$.   • (C) It converts 16-bit floating point numbers to 8-bit integers without overflow.
  • (D) It acts as a learning rate multiplier for the CLIP text encoder.

Exhaustive Latent Diffusion Analysis:

  • (B) is CORRECT: Standard diffusion theory (DDPM) is formulated on the fundamental mathematical assumption that clean data $\mathbf{x}_0$ is normalized to zero mean and unit variance ($\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_0] = 0, \operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{x}_0) \approx 1.0$). This ensures that when $\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ is formed with $\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, the total variance of $\mathbf{x}_t$ is strictly preserved at $1.0$ across all timesteps: $$\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{x}_t) = \bar{\alpha}_t \operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{x}_0) + (1 - \bar{\alpha}_t) \operatorname{Var}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}) = \bar{\alpha}_t(1) + (1 - \bar{\alpha}_t)(1) = 1.0$$ However, when the authors trained the VAE autoencoder, they used a very small KL regularization weight ($\beta \approx 10^{-6}$) to avoid blurry reconstructions. Code Lab 4.4 — DDPM Closed-Form Forward & Reverse Engine

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class DDPMScheduler:
    def __init__(self, timesteps=1000, beta_start=1e-4, beta_end=0.02):
        self.timesteps = timesteps
        # 1. Linear variance schedule
        self.betas = torch.linspace(beta_start, beta_end, timesteps)
        self.alphas = 1.0 - self.betas
        # Cumulative product: alpha_bar_t
        self.alphas_cumprod = torch.cumprod(self.alphas, dim=0)

    def q_sample(self, x_0, t, noise=None):
        """
        Forward process: Sample x_t directly from x_0 at timestep t
        x_t = sqrt(alpha_bar_t) * x_0 + sqrt(1 - alpha_bar_t) * eps
        """
        if noise is None:
            noise = torch.randn_like(x_0)

        sqrt_alpha_bar = torch.sqrt(self.alphas_cumprod[t]).view(-1, 1, 1, 1)
        sqrt_one_minus_alpha_bar = torch.sqrt(1.0 - self.alphas_cumprod[t]).view(-1, 1, 1, 1)

        return sqrt_alpha_bar * x_0 + sqrt_one_minus_alpha_bar * noise, noise

if __name__ == "__main__":
    scheduler = DDPMScheduler(timesteps=1000)
    x_0 = torch.ones(2, 3, 64, 64) # Batch of 2 pristine images
    t = torch.tensor([100, 900])   # Timestep 100 vs 900

    x_t, eps = scheduler.q_sample(x_0, t)
    print(f"Sampled x_t Shape: {x_t.shape}")
    print(f"Alpha_bar at t=100 : {scheduler.alphas_cumprod[100]:.4f} (Image mostly intact)")
    print(f"Alpha_bar at t=900 : {scheduler.alphas_cumprod[900]:.4f} (Pure Gaussian noise)")
