The AI revolution fractured this unified architecture into three distinct paradigms:

2. BERT: Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers

Introduced by Jacob Devlin and colleagues at Google in late 2018, BERT demonstrated that deep bidirectional conditioning produces far richer representations than left-to-right unidirectional models.

2.1 Input Representation & Special Tokens

BERT tokenizes input sequences using WordPiece. Every sequence is augmented with distinct architectural tokens:

  • [CLS] (Classification Token): Pre-pended as the very first token of every sequence. In the final encoder layer, the hidden state $\mathbf{h}_{[\text{CLS}]} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ aggregates sentence-level context and is fed into classification heads.
  • [SEP] (Separator Token): Appended at the boundary between sentence pairs $(A, B)$ or at the end of a single sequence.
  • [MASK]: Represents masked tokens during pre-training.

The input embedding for token $i$ is the element-wise sum of three independent embedding lookups:

$$\mathbf{x}_i = \mathbf{E}_{\text{token}}(t_i) + \mathbf{E}_{\text{position}}(i) + \mathbf{E}_{\text{segment}}(s_i)$$

where $\mathbf{E}_{\text{segment}}$ indicates whether the token belongs to Sentence $A$ or Sentence $B$.

2.2 Pre-training Objective 1: Masked Language Modeling (MLM)

Standard language models cannot train bidirectionally using next-token prediction, because in a multi-layer bidirectional Transformer, each word could trivially "see itself" in future layers, creating trivial identity shortcuts. To prevent this, BERT introduced the Cloze task (Masked Language Modeling):

  1. $15\%$ of all WordPiece tokens in a sequence are randomly selected for masking.
  2. To prevent a mismatch between pre-training (where [MASK] exists) and fine-tuning (where [MASK] never appears), the $15\%$ chosen tokens are treated as follows:
    • $80\%$ of the time: Replaced with the special [MASK] token (e.g., "my dog is [MASK]").
    • $10\%$ of the time: Replaced with a random vocabulary token (e.g., "my dog is apple"). This forces the encoder to maintain contextual representations for every token, since it cannot know whether a token was corrupted.
    • $10\%$ of the time: Kept unchanged (e.g., "my dog is hairy"). This biases the representation toward the actual observed token.

Let $\mathcal{M}$ be the set of masked indices. The MLM loss is the standard cross-entropy over the vocabulary computed only on the masked positions:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{MLM}}(\theta) = -\sum_{i \in \mathcal{M}} \log P(t_i = w_i^* | \mathbf{h}_i)$$

where $\mathbf{h}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the output of the final Transformer layer at position $i$, and $P(t_i | \mathbf{h}_i) = \text{Softmax}(W_{\text{vocab}} \mathbf{h}_i + \mathbf{b})$.

2.3 Pre-training Objective 2: Next Sentence Prediction (NSP)

To train the model to understand multi-sentence discourse (crucial for Question Answering and Natural Language Inference), BERT used a binary auxiliary loss. For training pairs $(A, B)$:

  • $50\%$ of the time, $B$ is the actual next sentence following $A$ in the corpus (labeled IsNext).
  • $50\%$ of the time, $B$ is a random sentence sampled from elsewhere in the corpus (labeled NotNext).
$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{NSP}} = -y \log \sigma(\mathbf{w}_{\text{NSP}}^\top \mathbf{h}_{[\text{CLS}]}) - (1-y) \log(1 - \sigma(\mathbf{w}_{\text{NSP}}^\top \mathbf{h}_{[\text{CLS}]}))$$

3. Evolution of Encoders: RoBERTa and DeBERTa

3.1 RoBERTa: A Robustly Optimized BERT Pretraining Approach (Liu et al., 2019)

Meta AI researchers conducted a rigorous ablation study of BERT's hyperparameters and discovered that the original BERT was severely undertrained. They introduced RoBERTa with four critical architectural and training changes:

  • Removal of NSP: Ablation proved that the Next Sentence Prediction task actually degraded downstream performance on sentence pairs when sequences were simply packed with contiguous text chunks. Removing NSP improved performance across GLUE and SQuAD.
  • Dynamic Masking: BERT generated a static masking pattern once during data preprocessing, meaning the model saw the exact same masked tokens across epochs. RoBERTa generated new masking patterns dynamically on the fly whenever a sequence was fed to the model.
  • Larger Mini-Batches: Scaled batch sizes from $256$ sequences up to $8,192$ sequences using distributed data parallelism, which stabilized optimization and sped up training.
  • Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE): Replaced BERT's $30,000$-token character-level WordPiece with a $50,000$-token byte-level BPE vocabulary (similar to GPT-2), removing out-of-vocabulary artifacts.

3.2 DeBERTa: Disentangled Attention & Enhanced Mask Decoder (He et al., 2020)

Microsoft's DeBERTa represents the pinnacle of encoder Transformer design, surpassing human baselines on SuperGLUE. DeBERTa introduces Disentangled Attention:

In standard BERT, each token is represented by a single vector summing content and absolute position: $\mathbf{x}_i = \mathbf{c}_i + \mathbf{p}_i$. DeBERTa represents each token with two separate vectors: content vector $\mathbf{c}_i$ and relative position vector $\mathbf{p}_{i|j}$. The attention score between token $i$ and token $j$ decomposes into four disentangled matrices:

$$A_{i,j} = \underbrace{\mathbf{c}_i^\top W_{q,c}^\top W_{k,c} \mathbf{c}_j}_{\text{content-to-content}} + \underbrace{\mathbf{c}_i^\top W_{q,c}^\top W_{k,r} \mathbf{p}_{i|j}}_{\text{content-to-position}} + \underbrace{\mathbf{p}_{j|i}^\top W_{q,r}^\top W_{k,c} \mathbf{c}_j}_{\text{position-to-content}} + \underbrace{\mathbf{p}_{j|i}^\top W_{q,r}^\top W_{k,r} \mathbf{p}_{i|j}}_{\text{position-to-position (approx 0)}}$$

This allows the model to capture how the semantic meaning of a word depends on its relative distance from grammatical modifiers with extreme precision.

4. Sentence-BERT (SBERT): Bi-Encoders vs Cross-Encoders

How do we use an encoder to find semantically similar documents in a database of $N = 10,000,000$ texts?

The $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ Cross-Encoder Bottleneck

The naive approach uses BERT as a Cross-Encoder: concatenate the query $q$ and document $d$ into a single sequence [CLS] q [SEP] d [SEP], feed it through 12 Transformer layers, and predict similarity via $\mathbf{h}_{[\text{CLS}]}$.

While the Cross-Encoder is exceptionally accurate because every token in the query attends to every token in the document via all-to-all cross-attention, its computational cost is catastrophic for retrieval:

  • To find the most similar document for a single query across $N = 10,000$ documents requires $10,000$ separate BERT forward passes ($\sim 10$ seconds on an A100).
  • To perform pairwise deduplication over $10,000$ sentences requires $\frac{10,000 \times 9,999}{2} \approx 50,000,000$ inference passes ($\approx 65$ hours of GPU compute!). For $10^7$ enterprise documents, this is mathematically impossible.

The Bi-Encoder Solution (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019)

Sentence-BERT (SBERT) resolves this via a Siamese Bi-Encoder network. SBERT passes query $u$ and document $v$ through identical (or shared) encoders separately, applies mean pooling over all token output vectors to obtain fixed-size sentence vectors $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, and computes semantic similarity via cosine distance:

$$\text{sim}(u, v) = \cos(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \frac{\mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_2 \|\mathbf{v}\|_2}$$

The Two-Stage Enterprise Retrieval Architecture:
Production RAG systems (Chapter 5.3) resolve the precision vs speed trade-off using a two-stage cascade:

  1. Stage 1 (Bi-Encoder Retrieval / MIPS): Pre-compute embeddings for all $10^8$ documents offline and index them in HNSW or FAISS. At query time, embed the query in $5$ ms and retrieve the top-$100$ candidate documents via Maximum Inner Product Search (MIPS) in sub-millisecond time.
  2. Stage 2 (Cross-Encoder Re-Ranking): Pass only the top-$100$ retrieved candidates through a heavy Cross-Encoder (e.g., bge-reranker-large), allowing full token-level cross-attention to re-order the candidates with 99th-percentile precision for the LLM context window.

6. Production Code Lab: Bi-Encoder with Multiple Negatives Ranking Loss (MNRL)

Below is a clean, production-grade PyTorch implementation of a Siamese Bi-Encoder trained with Multiple Negatives Ranking Loss (MNRL) — the gold-standard contrastive loss used to train models like OpenAI text-embedding-3, Cohere Embed, and BGE.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class MeanPooling(nn.Module):
    """Mean Pooling: aggregates token embeddings while strictly respecting the attention mask."""
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()

    def forward(self, token_embeddings: torch.Tensor, attention_mask: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        # Expand attention mask from (batch_size, seq_len) to (batch_size, seq_len, hidden_dim)
        input_mask_expanded = attention_mask.unsqueeze(-1).expand(token_embeddings.size()).float()
        
        # Sum token embeddings over sequence length
        sum_embeddings = torch.sum(token_embeddings * input_mask_expanded, dim=1)
        # Sum mask weights to get valid token count per sequence (clamped to avoid div-by-zero)
        sum_mask = torch.clamp(input_mask_expanded.sum(dim=1), min=1e-9)
        
        return sum_embeddings / sum_mask


class MultipleNegativesRankingLoss(nn.Module):
    """
    Multiple Negatives Ranking Loss (MNRL).
    For a batch of (query_i, pos_doc_i) pairs, all other pos_docs j != i in the batch
    serve as in-batch negative samples. Scales contrastive training effortlessly!
    """
    def __init__(self, scale: float = 20.0):
        super().__init__()
        self.scale = scale  # Temperature parameter (1 / tau)
        self.cross_entropy = nn.CrossEntropyLoss()

    def forward(self, query_embeddings: torch.Tensor, doc_embeddings: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        # Normalize embeddings to unit hypersphere
        q_norm = F.normalize(query_embeddings, p=2, dim=1)
        d_norm = F.normalize(doc_embeddings, p=2, dim=1)
        
        # Compute cosine similarity matrix: (batch_size, batch_size)
        # S[i, j] = cos(q_i, d_j) * scale
        similarity_matrix = torch.matmul(q_norm, d_norm.T) * self.scale
        
        # Ground truth labels: query_i corresponds to doc_i (diagonal entries)
        labels = torch.arange(query_embeddings.size(0), device=query_embeddings.device)
        
        # Cross entropy treats all off-diagonals as negative classes!
        loss = self.cross_entropy(similarity_matrix, labels)
        return loss


class ProductionBiEncoder(nn.Module):
    """Production Siamese Bi-Encoder for Dense Retrieval."""
    def __init__(self, encoder_backbone: nn.Module, embedding_dim: int = 768, proj_dim: int = 256):
        super().__init__()
        self.backbone = encoder_backbone
        self.pooler = MeanPooling()
        self.proj = nn.Linear(embedding_dim, proj_dim, bias=False)

    def encode(self, input_ids: torch.Tensor, attention_mask: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        # Backbone forward (e.g. HuggingFace Transformer)
        outputs = self.backbone(input_ids=input_ids, attention_mask=attention_mask)
        # Extract last hidden state: (batch_size, seq_len, hidden_dim)
        last_hidden = outputs.last_hidden_state if hasattr(outputs, 'last_hidden_state') else outputs[0]
        
        # Mean pool and project
        pooled = self.pooler(last_hidden, attention_mask)
        projected = self.proj(pooled)
        return F.normalize(projected, p=2, dim=1)

    def forward(self, q_ids, q_mask, d_ids, d_mask):
        q_emb = self.encode(q_ids, q_mask)
        d_emb = self.encode(d_ids, d_mask)
        return q_emb, d_emb

7. Capstone: Cross-Encoder Neural Re-Ranking Pipeline

Hands-on Capstone: Building a Cross-Encoder Re-ranker
Below is the production re-ranking pipeline that ingests top-$K$ candidates returned by a vector database and re-scores them via full cross-attention:

class CrossEncoderReranker(nn.Module):
    """
    Cross-Encoder scoring module.
    Takes concatenated [CLS] query [SEP] document [SEP] pairs and predicts relevance score.
    """
    def __init__(self, encoder_backbone: nn.Module, hidden_dim: int = 768):
        super().__init__()
        self.backbone = encoder_backbone
        self.classifier = nn.Linear(hidden_dim, 1)

    def forward(self, pair_input_ids: torch.Tensor, pair_attention_mask: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        # Full bidirectional cross-attention across query and doc tokens
        outputs = self.backbone(input_ids=pair_input_ids, attention_mask=pair_attention_mask)
        # Extract [CLS] representation at position 0
        cls_hidden = outputs.last_hidden_state[:, 0, :] if hasattr(outputs, 'last_hidden_state') else outputs[0][:, 0, :]
        
        # Output raw scalar relevance logit
        logits = self.classifier(cls_hidden).squeeze(-1)
        return logits

def rerank_candidates(query: str, retrieved_docs: list, reranker_fn, top_k: int = 5) -> list:
    """Re-ranks retrieved candidates by descending cross-encoder relevance score."""
    scores = reranker_fn(query, retrieved_docs)
    ranked_indices = sorted(range(len(scores)), key=lambda i: scores[i], reverse=True)
    return [(retrieved_docs[i], scores[i]) for i in ranked_indices[:top_k]]

Problem 5.1B.1 Medium Google Amazon

Masked Language Model (MLM) Cross-Entropy Loss & Gradient Step-by-Step:

Suppose an encoder model with hidden dimension $d = 3$ is pre-trained on a toy vocabulary of size $|V| = 5$:

$$V = \{0: \text{"London"}, \; 1: \text{"France"}, \; 2: \text{"Berlin"}, \; 3: \text{"Paris"}, \; 4: \text{"Rome"}\}$$

For the input sentence "The capital of France is Paris", the token at index 3 (ground truth word "France", class index $y = 1$) is masked with [MASK]. At this masked position, the final layer output vector is:

$$\mathbf{h}_3 = [1.2, -0.5, 0.8]^\top$$

The vocabulary projection weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{5 \times 3}$ and bias vector $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^5$ are:

$$W = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 1.0 & -0.2 \\ 1.0 & -1.0 & 0.5 \\ 0.2 & 0.0 & 1.0 \\ 0.8 & -0.4 & 0.2 \\ -0.5 & 0.5 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.1 \\ 0.2 \\ -0.3 \\ 0.0 \\ -0.1 \end{bmatrix}$$
  1. Calculate the unnormalized vocabulary logit vector $\mathbf{z} = W \mathbf{h}_3 + \mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^5$.
  2. Compute the Softmax probability distribution $\mathbf{p} = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{z})$.
  3. Calculate the Masked Language Modeling cross-entropy loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{MLM}} = -\ln(p_1)$.
  4. Compute the exact gradient vector of the loss with respect to the logit vector: $\nabla_{\mathbf{z}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{MLM}} = \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y}$.
Complete Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Unnormalized Logit Computation

Compute row-by-row inner products with $\mathbf{h}_3 = [1.2, -0.5, 0.8]^\top$ plus bias:

  • $z_0 = (0.5)(1.2) + (1.0)(-0.5) + (-0.2)(0.8) + 0.1 = 0.60 - 0.50 - 0.16 + 0.10 = \mathbf{0.04}$
  • $z_1 = (1.0)(1.2) + (-1.0)(-0.5) + (0.5)(0.8) + 0.2 = 1.20 + 0.50 + 0.40 + 0.20 = \mathbf{2.30}$
  • $z_2 = (0.2)(1.2) + (0.0)(-0.5) + (1.0)(0.8) - 0.3 = 0.24 + 0.00 + 0.80 - 0.30 = \mathbf{0.74}$
  • $z_3 = (0.8)(1.2) + (-0.4)(-0.5) + (0.2)(0.8) + 0.0 = 0.96 + 0.20 + 0.16 + 0.00 = \mathbf{1.32}$
  • $z_4 = (-0.5)(1.2) + (0.5)(-0.5) + (0.5)(0.8) - 0.1 = -0.60 - 0.25 + 0.40 - 0.10 = \mathbf{-0.55}$
$$\mathbf{z} = [0.04, \; 2.30, \; 0.74, \; 1.32, \; -0.55]^\top$$

Step 2: Softmax Probabilities

Compute the exponentials:

$$e^{0.04} \approx 1.04081, \quad e^{2.30} \approx 9.97418, \quad e^{0.74} \approx 2.09594, \quad e^{1.32} \approx 3.74342, \quad e^{-0.55} \approx 0.57695$$

The normalizer sum is:

$$\Sigma = 1.04081 + 9.97418 + 2.09594 + 3.74342 + 0.57695 = 17.43130$$

Dividing each exponential by $\Sigma$:

$$p_0 = \frac{1.04081}{17.43130} \approx \mathbf{0.0597}$$ $$p_1 = \frac{9.97418}{17.43130} \approx \mathbf{0.5722}$$ $$p_2 = \frac{2.09594}{17.43130} \approx \mathbf{0.1202}$$ $$p_3 = \frac{3.74342}{17.43130} \approx \mathbf{0.2148}$$ $$p_4 = \frac{0.57695}{17.43130} \approx \mathbf{0.0331}$$

Probability check: $0.0597 + 0.5722 + 0.1202 + 0.2148 + 0.0331 = 1.0000$.

Step 3: MLM Cross-Entropy Loss

The ground-truth target is class $y = 1$ ("France"):

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{MLM}} = -\ln(p_1) = -\ln(0.5722) \approx \mathbf{0.5583}$$

Step 4: Analytical Gradient $\nabla_{\mathbf{z}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{MLM}}$

The ground truth one-hot target is $\mathbf{y} = [0, 1, 0, 0, 0]^\top$.

$$\nabla_{\mathbf{z}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{MLM}} = \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.0597 - 0 \\ 0.5722 - 1 \\ 0.1202 - 0 \\ 0.2148 - 0 \\ 0.0331 - 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0.0597} \\ \mathbf{-0.4278} \\ \mathbf{0.1202} \\ \mathbf{0.2148} \\ \mathbf{0.0331} \end{bmatrix}$$

The negative gradient on logit 1 pulls its prediction higher toward 1, while positive gradients push all other distractors down toward zero.

Problem 5.1B.2 Medium Meta Apple

SBERT Mask-Aware Mean Pooling & The Padding Corruption Trap:

Consider a mini-batch of text fed into a 3-dimensional encoder. For Sentence $A$, the sequence length padded to $T = 4$ has attention mask vector $\mathbf{m} = [1, 1, 1, 0]^\top$ (where the 4th token is zero-padded [PAD]). The final layer token embedding matrix $H \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 3}$ is:

$$\mathbf{h}_1 = [1.0, 2.0, 0.0]^\top, \quad \mathbf{h}_2 = [0.0, 1.0, 2.0]^\top, \quad \mathbf{h}_3 = [2.0, 0.0, 1.0]^\top, \quad \mathbf{h}_4 = [-99.0, 50.0, 12.0]^\top$$
  1. Calculate the true mask-aware Mean Pooled sentence embedding vector $\mathbf{u} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^4 m_t \mathbf{h}_t}{\sum_{t=1}^4 m_t}$.
  2. Calculate the corrupted sentence vector $\mathbf{u}_{\text{naive}}$ if a software engineer mistakenly uses PyTorch's native torch.mean(H, dim=0) without masking.
  3. Suppose a pre-computed target query vector is $\mathbf{v} = [1.0, 1.0, 1.0]^\top$. Calculate the exact cosine similarity $\cos(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v})$ under true mask-aware pooling versus $\cos(\mathbf{u}_{\text{naive}}, \mathbf{v})$ under naive unmasked pooling.
  4. State the percentage distortion in semantic similarity caused by the padding artifact.
Complete Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Mask-Aware Mean Pooling

Using the binary attention mask $\mathbf{m} = [1, 1, 1, 0]^\top$, token 4 is completely zeroed out:

$$\sum_{t=1}^4 m_t \mathbf{h}_t = 1 \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1.0 \\ 2.0 \\ 0.0 \end{bmatrix} + 1 \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 0.0 \\ 1.0 \\ 2.0 \end{bmatrix} + 1 \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 2.0 \\ 0.0 \\ 1.0 \end{bmatrix} + 0 \cdot \mathbf{h}_4 = \begin{bmatrix} 3.0 \\ 3.0 \\ 3.0 \end{bmatrix}$$

The number of valid non-padding tokens is $\sum_{t=1}^4 m_t = 1 + 1 + 1 + 0 = 3$.

$$\mathbf{u} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 3.0 \\ 3.0 \\ 3.0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{1.0} \\ \mathbf{1.0} \\ \mathbf{1.0} \end{bmatrix}$$

Step 2: Naive Unmasked Mean Pooling

Summing all 4 vectors including the garbage padding vector $\mathbf{h}_4$:

$$\sum_{t=1}^4 \mathbf{h}_t = \begin{bmatrix} 3.0 - 99.0 \\ 3.0 + 50.0 \\ 3.0 + 12.0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -96.0 \\ 53.0 \\ 15.0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Dividing by $T = 4$:

$$\mathbf{u}_{\text{naive}} = \frac{1}{4} \begin{bmatrix} -96.0 \\ 53.0 \\ 15.0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{-24.00} \\ \mathbf{13.25} \\ \mathbf{3.75} \end{bmatrix}$$

Step 3: Cosine Similarity Comparison

Target vector is $\mathbf{v} = [1.0, 1.0, 1.0]^\top$.

1. True Mask-Aware Vector $\mathbf{u} = [1, 1, 1]^\top$:

$$\cos(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_2 \|\mathbf{v}\|_2} = \frac{1(1) + 1(1) + 1(1)}{\sqrt{1^2+1^2+1^2} \sqrt{1^2+1^2+1^2}} = \frac{3}{\sqrt{3}\sqrt{3}} = \frac{3}{3} = \mathbf{1.0000}$$

The sentence has perfect semantic alignment with the query ($\cos = 1.0$).

2. Corrupted Vector $\mathbf{u}_{\text{naive}} = [-24.0, 13.25, 3.75]^\top$:

$$\mathbf{u}_{\text{naive}} \cdot \mathbf{v} = -24.0(1) + 13.25(1) + 3.75(1) = -24.0 + 17.0 = -7.0$$ $$\|\mathbf{u}_{\text{naive}}\|_2 = \sqrt{(-24)^2 + 13.25^2 + 3.75^2} = \sqrt{576 + 175.5625 + 14.0625} = \sqrt{765.625} \approx 27.670$$ $$\|\mathbf{v}\|_2 = \sqrt{3} \approx 1.73205$$ $$\cos(\mathbf{u}_{\text{naive}}, \mathbf{v}) = \frac{-7.0}{27.670 \times 1.73205} = \frac{-7.0}{47.926} \approx \mathbf{-0.1461}$$

Step 4: Percentage Distortion & Impact

A query that has identical meaning ($\cos = 1.000$) is transformed into a negatively correlated vector ($\cos = -0.146$) solely because padding tokens were included in the average! This proves why masking in pooling layers is non-negotiable in production SBERT implementations.

Problem 5.1B.3 Hard Microsoft Elastic

Two-Stage Neural Retrieval Latency & Memory Sizing Audit:

An enterprise knowledge base contains $N = 1,000,000$ internal documentation articles. Average query length is $L_q = 32$ tokens, and average document chunk length is $L_d = 256$ tokens. You must evaluate two search architectures on an NVIDIA A100 GPU cluster:

Mathematical Proof that BERT MLM Maximizes the Lower Bound on Contextual Mutual Information:

Let $\mathbf{W} = (W_1, \dots, W_N)$ be a sequence of random variables representing words in a sentence drawn from language distribution $P(\mathbf{W})$.

  1. Show why the standard autoregressive decomposition $P(\mathbf{W}) = \prod_{t=1}^N P(W_t | W_{
  2. In Masked Language Modeling, a token index $i \in \{1, \dots, N\}$ is uniformly chosen and masked. Write the MLM objective $\mathcal{J}_{\text{MLM}}$ as the expected log-likelihood of predicting the masked token $W_i$ conditioned on all remaining words $\mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}}$.
  3. Define the Mutual Information $I(W_i; \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}})$ between token $W_i$ and its bidirectional context.
  4. Prove that maximizing the Masked Language Modeling objective directly maximizes a variational lower bound on the average mutual information between each token and its surrounding bidirectional sentence: $$\mathcal{J}_{\text{MLM}} \ge \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N I(W_i; \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}}) - H(W_i)$$
Complete Step-by-Step Proof:

Step 1: The Asymmetry of Autoregressive Modeling

By the chain rule of probability, any joint distribution can be factorized along an arbitrary permutation $\pi$:

$$P(\mathbf{W}) = \prod_{t=1}^N P(W_{\pi(t)} | W_{\pi(Standard left-to-right causal models select the canonical order $\pi(t) = t$. While exact for generative sampling, for sentence understanding it artificially forces token $W_t$ to be conditionally independent of future tokens $W_{t+1}, \dots, W_N$ given the past, preventing the model from resolving syntactic ambiguities where the semantic disambiguator appears at the end of the sentence (e.g., "The bank was flooded with water" vs "The bank approved the loan").

Step 2: The Masked Language Modeling Objective

Let $i \sim \text{Uniform}(1, \dots, N)$. The MLM training objective parameterized by network weights $\theta$ is:

$$\mathcal{J}_{\text{MLM}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{E}_{P(W_i, \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}})} \left[ \log Q_\theta(W_i | \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}}) \right]$$

where $Q_\theta(W_i | \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}})$ is the model's Softmax output over vocabulary $V$.

Step 3: Definition of Contextual Mutual Information

The Mutual Information between the masked token $W_i$ and its surrounding context $\mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}}$ is:

$$I(W_i; \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}}) = H(W_i) - H(W_i | \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}})$$

where $H(W_i) = -\sum_{w} P(W_i = w) \log P(W_i = w)$ is the marginal entropy of token $W_i$, and $H(W_i | \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}})$ is the conditional entropy:

$$H(W_i | \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}}) = -\mathbb{E}_{P(W_i, \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}})} \left[ \log P(W_i | \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}}) \right]$$

Step 4: Deriving the Variational Lower Bound

Consider the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the true posterior $P(W_i | \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}})$ and the model distribution $Q_\theta(W_i | \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}})$:

$$D_{\text{KL}}\left( P(W_i | \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}}) \parallel Q_\theta(W_i | \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}}) \right) \ge 0$$

Expanding the KL divergence:

$$\mathbb{E}_{P} \left[ \log \frac{P(W_i | \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}})}{Q_\theta(W_i | \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}})} \right] \ge 0$$ $$\mathbb{E}_{P} \left[ \log P(W_i | \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}}) \right] - \mathbb{E}_{P} \left[ \log Q_\theta(W_i | \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}}) \right] \ge 0$$ $$-H(W_i | \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}}) - \mathbb{E}_{P} \left[ \log Q_\theta(W_i | \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}}) \right] \ge 0$$

Rearranging terms:

$$\mathbb{E}_{P} \left[ \log Q_\theta(W_i | \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}}) \right] \le -H(W_i | \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}})$$

Substitute $H(W_i | \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}}) = H(W_i) - I(W_i; \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}})$:

$$\mathbb{E}_{P} \left[ \log Q_\theta(W_i | \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}}) \right] \le I(W_i; \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}}) - H(W_i)$$

Averaging over all positions $i \in \{1, \dots, N\}$, we find that the negative cross-entropy loss satisfies:

$$\mathcal{J}_{\text{MLM}}(\theta) = -\mathcal{L}_{\text{MLM}}(\theta) \ge \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N I(W_i; \mathbf{W}_{\setminus \{i\}}) - H(W_i) \quad \blacksquare$$

Maximizing the MLM objective directly tightens the variational lower bound, forcing the Transformer's internal bidirectional attention weights to capture the maximum possible statistical dependency between each word and its surrounding sentence.

First-Principles Mathematical Derivation of DeBERTa Disentangled Attention:

In standard BERT, the input representation of token $i$ is the direct sum of content embedding $\mathbf{c}_i$ and absolute position embedding $\mathbf{p}_i$: $\mathbf{x}_i = \mathbf{c}_i + \mathbf{p}_i$.

  1. Express the unscaled attention score $A_{i,j} = \mathbf{x}_i W_Q W_K^\top \mathbf{x}_j^\top$ and expand it into four separate bilinear matrix products.
  2. Identify why the fourth term ($\mathbf{p}_i W_Q W_K^\top \mathbf{p}_j^\top$) provides virtually zero semantic signal in natural language.
  3. In DeBERTa (He et al., 2020), content and relative positions are completely disentangled. Let $\mathbf{p}_{i|j}$ denote the relative distance vector from token $i$ to token $j$. Show how DeBERTa uses four independent projection matrices $W_{q,c}, W_{k,c}, W_{q,r}, W_{k,r}$ to construct the 3-term disentangled attention score: $$A_{i,j} = \mathbf{c}_i W_{q,c} W_{k,c}^\top \mathbf{c}_j^\top + \mathbf{c}_i W_{q,c} W_{k,r}^\top \mathbf{p}_{i|j}^\top + \mathbf{p}_{j|i} W_{q,r} W_{k,c}^\top \mathbf{c}_j^\top$$
  4. Explain the linguistic meaning of the "content-to-position" vs "position-to-content" terms.
Complete Step-by-Step Derivation:

Step 1: Expansion of Standard BERT Attention

Substituting $\mathbf{x}_i = \mathbf{c}_i + \mathbf{p}_i$ and $\mathbf{x}_j = \mathbf{c}_j + \mathbf{p}_j$ into the bilinear attention product:

$$A_{i,j} = (\mathbf{c}_i + \mathbf{p}_i) W_Q W_K^\top (\mathbf{c}_j + \mathbf{p}_j)^\top$$

Expanding the matrix product by distributivity:

$$A_{i,j} = \underbrace{\mathbf{c}_i W_Q W_K^\top \mathbf{c}_j^\top}_{\text{content-to-content}} + \underbrace{\mathbf{c}_i W_Q W_K^\top \mathbf{p}_j^\top}_{\text{content-to-position}} + \underbrace{\mathbf{p}_i W_Q W_K^\top \mathbf{c}_j^\top}_{\text{position-to-content}} + \underbrace{\mathbf{p}_i W_Q W_K^\top \mathbf{p}_j^\top}_{\text{position-to-position}}$$

Step 2: Pathology of the Position-to-Position Term

The fourth term $\mathbf{p}_i W_Q W_K^\top \mathbf{p}_j^\top$ depends only on where the words are located in the sequence, completely ignoring what the words actually mean. For instance, it says "token at position 3 should attend to token at position 5" regardless of whether the sentence is a question, a legal contract, or code. In natural text, absolute position-to-position bias is largely redundant and adds substantial noise. DeBERTa explicitly drops this term, setting it to zero.

Step 3: DeBERTa Disentangled Projection Formulation

Rather than forcing content and position vectors to share the single projection matrices $W_Q$ and $W_K$, DeBERTa defines dedicated projections:

  • Content Query: $\mathbf{q}_i^c = \mathbf{c}_i W_{q,c}$
  • Content Key: $\mathbf{k}_j^c = \mathbf{c}_j W_{k,c}$
  • Relative Position Query: $\mathbf{q}_{j|i}^r = \mathbf{p}_{j|i} W_{q,r}$
  • Relative Position Key: $\mathbf{k}_{i|j}^r = \mathbf{p}_{i|j} W_{k,r}$

The unscaled attention score is formulated as the sum of three disentangled components:

$$A_{i,j} = \underbrace{\mathbf{q}_i^c (\mathbf{k}_j^c)^\top}_{\text{content-to-content}} + \underbrace{\mathbf{q}_i^c (\mathbf{k}_{i|j}^r)^\top}_{\text{content-to-position}} + \underbrace{\mathbf{q}_{j|i}^r (\mathbf{k}_j^c)^\top}_{\text{position-to-content}} \quad \blacksquare$$

Step 4: Linguistic Intuition of Disentangled Terms

  • Content-to-Content ($\mathbf{q}_i^c (\mathbf{k}_j^c)^\top$): Captures pure semantic co-reference (e.g., word "apple" relates to word "fruit" regardless of where they appear).
  • Content-to-Position ($\mathbf{q}_i^c (\mathbf{k}_{i|j}^r)^\top$): Evaluates: "Given that I am the verb 'threw', how important is the token located 2 positions to my right?" (likely the direct object noun).
  • Position-to-Content ($\mathbf{q}_{j|i}^r (\mathbf{k}_j^c)^\top$): Evaluates: "Given that a token is located 1 position to my left, how does its specific content (e.g., adjective 'heavy') modify me?"

This disentanglement allows DeBERTa to model syntax and grammatical dependency parsing with unprecedented mathematical precision.

Chapter 5.1B Key Takeaways

  • Encoder Architecture: Encoders employ unrestricted bidirectional self-attention and are optimized via Masked Language Modeling (MLM), making them ideal for sentence embeddings, classification, and retrieval.
  • BERT: Trained on 15% masked tokens with WordPiece tokenization; maps words into rich context-dependent representations.
  • RoBERTa Improvements: Proved that BERT was undertrained, eliminated the detrimental NSP task, implemented dynamic masking, and scaled batch sizes.
  • DeBERTa Disentanglement: Decoupled content and relative position vectors, computing content-to-content, content-to-position, and position-to-content attention for human-level NLU.
  • SBERT Siamese Networks: Solved the $O(N^2)$ cross-encoder bottleneck by pre-computing fixed-size sentence vectors via mask-aware mean pooling.
  • Two-Stage Enterprise Search: High-throughput Bi-Encoder MIPS for candidate recall ($K=100$) followed by a heavy Cross-Encoder for precision re-ranking ($K=10$).
  • Multiple Negatives Ranking Loss (MNRL): Efficient in-batch contrastive loss that treats all other documents in a mini-batch as negative samples, scaling dense retrieval training.
